What do the EICAT and SEICAT categories mean?

EICAT rates the documented harm a species has done to native species where it is already alien; SEICAT rates the harm to people's activities, on the same five steps. Neither predicts the impact here, and neither enters the risk score. A filled badge comes from GIDIAS impact records; a dashed badge marked LLM was proposed by a language model. What this shows

For a chosen SInAS location, the model scores every candidate species that has no recorded alien or native presence there, and ranks them by how likely they are to establish as an alien. Candidates are species already alien somewhere else (in SInAS). Each score comes with an uncertainty estimate, the features that pushed it up or down, and, for the top species, a map of the graph neighbours behind its relational score.

Read the risk score as a rank. It orders the species of one location. It is the model's output under its training mix of known invasions and sampled unrecorded pairs, not a calibrated probability of establishment, and it should not be compared between locations.

Data used

  • SInAS alien and native records per location. Alien records are the positive labels.
  • GloBI interactions between species, and with genera, families, orders and classes, grouped into resource, enemy, mutualism and competition links.
  • GBIF occurrences: where each species is recorded, and the taxonomy (species up to kingdom) that links species in the graph.
  • Location features: WorldClim climate including within-location extremes, Köppen–Geiger climate classes, geography, GDP, population density, colonial history and native species richness.
  • BACI trade (CEPII, HS 1992, 1995–2024), in 11 pathway channels such as plants for planting, live animals, wood packaging and used tyres, for the full period and for 2015–2024.

How the model works

Seven streams are combined by one classifier:

  • Relational: a two-layer graph neural network learns an embedding per species from the interaction and taxonomy graph. It is matched against a compressed profile of all location features.
  • Climate niche and per-axis suitability (two streams, one block in the charts): the species' realised climate niche (all locations where it is known to occur, native and alien, from SInAS and GBIF) compared with the target, as a whole, axis by axis, and as Köppen–Geiger class overlap.
  • Trade: imports of the target, trade flowing into it from the species' known range, and how strong an exporter that range is.
  • Biotic: the share of the species' helpful partners (food, hosts, mutualists) and harmful partners (predators, parasites, competitors) already present.
  • Location: properties of the target itself, such as island status, wealth and native richness.
  • Spread: how close the species' nearest known population is, and how many known locations lie within 1 000 km.

The scored location is always left out of the species' own range, so the model learns to predict new records, not to recognise existing ones. Species already recorded in the location are never listed. Whether a climate gate scaled other streams by the climate match is a training setting; the model card above names it.

Native-range and GRIIS checks

Before a list is cut to its length, two checks are applied to the ranking. Species that are native in the location (SInAS, or an extra checklist) and species already on the GRIIS checklist of the location's country (the Global Register of Introduced and Invasive Species) are removed: they are not horizon species. Removed species are replaced by the next candidates and are listed under "Checks" above the filters when the checks files are loaded.

Some findings are kept and flagged next to the species name instead: a species native elsewhere in the same country (for example on the mainland when an island group is scanned), and a GRIIS-listed species for a location that shares its country code with other locations, because GRIIS cannot say which of them is meant. Use the "Checklist flags" filter to show or hide them.

Feature contributions

The detail panel uses Integrated Gradients on the model's pre-sigmoid score. The baseline is an average candidate pair. Contributions add up to the difference between the species' score and the baseline score. Red bars raise the risk; blue bars lower it. They are grouped in six blocks: climate, trade, biotic, relational, spread and location. Contributions show what drives this model's score; they do not prove a cause.

GNN saliency map

The relational block is one number for the whole species embedding. The saliency map breaks it down over the graph: for each node within two steps of the species (its genus, interaction partners, and their neighbours), it shows how strongly the score depends on that node (gradient × input). The species sits in the centre, direct neighbours on the inner ring and neighbours of neighbours on the outer ring. Red nodes raise the score, blue nodes lower it, and node size shows the size of the effect. Hover a node, or read the list below the map, for the path that links it to the species.

How uncertainty is calculated

The ± value combines an ensemble of several models trained with different random seeds and Monte Carlo dropout: each model keeps its dropout layers on and scores each species 30 times.

  • Risk score is the mean output over all passes of all models.
  • Uncertainty (±) is the standard deviation of those outputs, in percentage points. The charts and the maximum-uncertainty filter use it. The detail panel also shows the part that comes from disagreement between the models.
  • Standard error is the standard deviation divided by √passes. It is the uncertainty_se column of the scan file.

This covers the model's own uncertainty, not gaps in the input data. Data support, below, covers those.

Data support and effort shift

Some species are far better documented than others, and documentation partly follows invasion: known invaders get studied. The model therefore scores every species as if it were documented like a typical known invader (GloBI records and partner counts, and whether the native range is known, are set to typical values). It also learned from negatives matched to the positives on documentation level, taxonomic group and location.

  • Data support (0–1) says how much evidence a prediction rests on. It averages three parts: the size of the known range, the number of known interaction partners (both count as full from 20 upward), and whether the native range is known. Low is below 0.4, high from 0.7. It does not change the score. A species with a high score and low support is a candidate to check, not a confident prediction.
  • Effort shift is the score after the effort correction minus the score the same model gives with the species' actual documentation, in percentage points. Negative: documentation alone would have ranked the species higher. Positive: it was held back by being poorly studied.

Scan files without these columns are still read; the columns then show “–”.

Documented impact: EICAT, SEICAT and where they come from

The risk score says how likely a species is to establish. How much harm it could do is a separate question, and this viewer keeps the two apart: impact data is never used by the model. It is shown next to each species so that the people assessing a list can weigh it themselves.

EICAT (Environmental Impact Classification for Alien Taxa) rates harm to native species; SEICAT (Socio-Economic Impact Classification of Alien Taxa) rates harm to people's activities. Both describe documented impacts where the species is already alien; they do not predict the impact in the selected location. The categories are read from species_impacts_master.csv, written by notebook 07_species_impacts.ipynb. Each category names its source:

  • MO GIDIAS records (filled badge): the highest impact magnitude that any publication in the Global Impacts Dataset of Invasive Alien Species reports for the species, with the number of records and sources behind it. It is a summary of evidence, not an IUCN assessment: one source can set the category, and because GIDIAS does not record reversibility a local extinction is shown as Major and may be Massive.
  • MOLLM LLM-derived (dashed badge): for a species GIDIAS does not cover, proposed by a language model that was asked for the same fields. It comes with the model's name, the agreement between repeated answers and a short justification. A language model can be confidently wrong and can cite sources that do not exist. Use it to decide which species to look at first.
  • NE Not evaluated: no impact data from either source.

The EICAT and the SEICAT category of one species can come from different sources. The "EICAT source" filter shows one kind only; in the chart, LLM-derived categories are drawn as hollow symbols. If notebook 07 compared its model with GIDIAS, the agreement is shown in the model card above. List files written before notebook 07 took over carry EICAT columns of their own; those are shown when no impact file is loaded.
